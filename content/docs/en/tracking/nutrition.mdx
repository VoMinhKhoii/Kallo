The dashboard handles calories and macros. The Nutrition page handles everything
else: fibre, minerals and vitamins — **28 figures** in total, tracked across a
period.

## How to read it

Each nutrient is a row, showing how much you reached against a reference target
over the period you selected.

But there is something more important than the percentage.

## Completeness and confidence

Not every day deserves to be in the calculation.

**Completeness** decides which days count. A day where you logged breakfast and
then forgot the rest would make every nutrient look severely deficient — when
really you are missing data, not nutrients. Kallo classifies days and leaves the
incomplete ones out.

**Confidence** tells you how firm the conclusion is. It depends on how many
complete days there are and how well the food composition data covers what you
ate.

<Callout label="Why micronutrients are harder than macros">
Protein and carbohydrate data is fairly complete across most food composition
tables. Vitamin and mineral data is not — many rows leave those columns empty.
A nutrient showing low may mean you ate too little of it, or that the reference
data has no figure. The confidence indicator exists so you can tell the two
apart.
</Callout>

This is why the page speaks in conditional language rather than handing you
verdicts.

## Nutrients worth attention

Kallo highlights the two you are shortest on, with food suggestions that would
close the gap — chosen from what actually appears in your meals, not a generic
list.

## Macro trends

The page also charts macros over time, useful when you want to see whether your
protein ratio is holding steady rather than just watching total calories.

## Choosing a period

Short periods react quickly but are noisy; long periods are steadier but slower
to reflect change. For micronutrients, longer is usually more useful — nobody
hits every nutrient every day, and nobody needs to.
